# gplab

**Gaussian processes** from scratch in NumPy (no SciPy): kernels with analytic hyper-parameter gradients, exact GP regression, the three classic sparse approximations (VFE, FITC, DTC), random-Fourier and Nystrom feature GPs, closed-form leave-one-out, and six studies that measure *when each approximation is good enough and where it misleads*. 62 tests; every number below is regenerated by `make_results.py` (about a minute on 2 CPU cores, raw results in `results/*.json`).

Everything is synthetic with a known generating process, so recovery and calibration can be scored against the truth. Read the numbers as a controlled lab, not as a claim about real data.

## What is in it

| Piece | Where | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| `RBF`, `Matern32`, `Matern52` (iso/ARD), `Periodic`, `SpectralMixture`, `Linear`, `+` / `*` | `kernels.py` | covariance functions whose `grad(X)` gives `dK/d(log param)` for every parameter; sums and products use the sum/product rules, so `Periodic * RBF + Linear` is fit with exact gradients |
| `ExactGP` | `gp.py` | Cholesky GP: log marginal likelihood **and its analytic gradient** (`0.5 tr((a a^T - K^-1) dK)`), multi-restart L-BFGS fitting, predictive mean/var/covariance, posterior and prior samples, **closed-form leave-one-out** (no refits) |
| `SparseGP` | `sparse.py` | one collapsed O(n m^2) implementation of **VFE** (Titsias bound, with the trace term), **FITC** (per-point noise) and **DTC**; inducing points by greedy pivoted-Cholesky (`greedy_inducing`) or k-means |
| `RandomFourierFeatures`, `NystromFeatures`, `FeatureGP` | `features.py` | RFF for RBF (Gaussian spectrum) and Matern 3/2, 5/2 (Student-t spectrum), optional orthogonal blocks; Nystrom map; Bayesian linear regression on features (O(n D^2)) |
| `lbfgs` | `optim.py` | small L-BFGS with Armijo backtracking and box bounds, so fitting needs only NumPy |
| studies + CLI | `studies.py`, `__main__.py` | `python -m gplab {hyper,sparse,features,calibration,extrapolation,scaling}` |

## Correctness checks (tests)

- Every kernel gradient (iso, ARD, periodic, linear, nested sums and products) against central finite differences, **and a deliberately scaled gradient is shown to fail the same check**. Kernels are symmetric PSD, and match hand-computed values.
- The marginal likelihood equals the dense multivariate-normal formula; its gradient equals finite differences through the whole Cholesky path; predictions equal the dense formula; closed-form LOO equals brute-force refitting for every point; posterior and prior samples have the right moments.
- Sparse: with `Z = X` VFE, FITC and DTC all recover the exact GP (bound, mean and variance); the collapsed VFE bound equals the explicit dense expression `log N(y|0,Q+s2 I) - tr(K-Q)/(2 s2)`; VFE is a lower bound on the exact lml for random inducing sets and increases with nested inducing sets; greedy selection really picks the largest remaining variance and beats random on `tr(K-Q)`; FITC's noise is heteroscedastic and DTC's is not; FITC underestimates the noise relative to VFE on a fixed problem.
- Features: RFF kernel error shrinks like `1/sqrt(D)`, is unbiased, converges for Matern 3/2 and 5/2, **and the wrong spectral density (Gaussian for a Matern kernel) is shown to be rejected**; orthogonal blocks are orthogonal and lower the error; Nystrom with `Z = X` is the exact kernel and the exact GP.
- Optimiser: Rosenbrock, an ill-conditioned quadratic, and box bounds.

```python
import numpy as np

X, y, _ = sample_gp_data(RBF(0.8, 1.5), 400, 0.05, seed=0)
gp = ExactGP(RBF(1.0, 1.0), 0.5).fit(X, y, restarts=3)      # maximise the marginal likelihood
print(np.exp(gp.params))                                     # lengthscale, variance, noise
mean, var = gp.predict(np.linspace(-3, 3, 100)[:, None], include_noise=True)
print(gp.loo_log_predictive())                               # leave-one-out, no refitting

Z = X[greedy_inducing(gp.kernel, X, 20)]                     # 20 inducing points
sp = SparseGP(RBF(1.0, 1.0), Z, 0.5, "vfe").fit(X, y)        # O(n m^2) instead of O(n^3)
print(sp.bound(), "<=", gp.log_marginal_likelihood())
```

## Results

### 1. Marginal-likelihood fitting recovers noise and lengthscale, but not the signal variance (`hyper`)

Data drawn from `RBF(l=0.7, var=1.5)` plus noise 0.1 on `[-3, 3]`; 8 seeds per size, 7 L-BFGS starts each (one at `[1, 1, 0.5]` plus 6 random log-space perturbations). Median [quartiles] of the maximum-likelihood estimates:

| n | lengthscale (true 0.7) | signal variance (true 1.5) | noise (true 0.1) | restarts in best basin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 0.79 [0.62, 0.83] | 0.96 [0.82, 1.98] | 0.08 [0.07, 0.10] | 0.84 |
| 50 | 0.73 [0.68, 0.83] | 1.80 [1.45, 1.94] | 0.09 [0.09, 0.11] | 0.96 |
| 100 | 0.69 [0.65, 0.74] | 1.29 [0.84, 1.65] | 0.10 [0.10, 0.10] | 1.00 |
| 300 | 0.66 [0.63, 0.74] | 0.96 [0.72, 1.91] | 0.10 [0.10, 0.10] | 1.00 |

Noise and lengthscale tighten with `n`; the signal variance does not. A GP draw on an interval six lengthscales wide contains only a handful of independent "bumps", so its amplitude is estimated from a few effective samples no matter how densely it is observed (the interquartile range at n=300 is still 0.72-1.91). With few points (n=20) about one restart in six ends in a worse basin; from n=100 all restarts agree, and no seed returned the flat "everything is noise-free smooth" optimum.

### 2. VFE, FITC and DTC against the exact GP (`sparse`)

n=1500 points of `sin(1.5x) + 0.4 sin(5x) + 0.2x` with noise variance 0.09 on `[-4, 4]`, greedy inducing points (chosen with the initial kernel), kernel and noise fit by L-BFGS on each method's own objective (finite-difference gradients, `Z` fixed). The exact GP reaches lml -357.3, learned noise 0.088, RMSE 0.0495 against the noise-free truth, NLPD 0.295. The greedy rule stops once the residual variance is numerically zero, so the request for 32 points used 26.

| M | method | objective | learned noise | learned lengthscale | RMSE vs truth | NLPD |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | vfe | -1324.4 | 0.328 | 1.13 | 0.489 | 0.85 |
| 4 | fitc | -1254.2 | 0.165 | 0.58 | 0.493 | 0.83 |
| 4 | dtc | -1266.5 | 0.312 | 0.71 | 0.486 | 0.92 |
| 8 | vfe | -747.4 | 0.152 | 2.56 | 0.277 | 0.54 |
| 8 | fitc | -747.0 | 0.152 | 2.52 | 0.277 | 0.54 |
| 12 | vfe | -713.3 | 0.140 | 1.42 | 0.253 | 0.52 |
| 12 | fitc | -498.4 | 0.072 | 0.29 | 0.184 | 0.39 |
| 12 | dtc | -496.3 | 0.108 | 0.28 | 0.179 | 0.54 |
| 16 | vfe | -393.9 | 0.091 | 0.69 | 0.065 | 0.31 |
| 16 | fitc | -375.0 | 0.085 | 0.59 | 0.066 | 0.31 |
| 24 | vfe | -358.4 | 0.088 | 0.55 | 0.049 | 0.30 |
| 24 | fitc | -356.9 | 0.087 | 0.50 | 0.049 | 0.30 |
| 26 | vfe | -357.5 | 0.088 | 0.52 | 0.049 | 0.30 |

(DTC is omitted where it tracks FITC: at M = 8, 16, 24, 26 its RMSE is within 0.0003 of FITC's and its noise within 0.005; M=12 and 4 are shown. All rows are in `results/sparse.json`.)

- **From about 24 inducing points all three methods are indistinguishable from the exact GP** (RMSE 0.049, same noise, same lengthscale to within 0.03). Below the information the data needs, they differ.
- **VFE is the conservative one.** At M=4 and 12 it explains the unresolved structure as noise (0.33 and 0.14 against a true 0.09) and a long lengthscale; FITC and DTC instead shrink the lengthscale to 0.3, which gives a *better* RMSE at M=12 (0.18 vs 0.25). FITC's learned noise (0.072 at M=12, 0.085 at M=16) sits below VFE's every time it differs, the known FITC tendency to under-estimate noise. Note this is one data set and one optimiser start per cell, so the M=12 gap shows a local-optimum difference, not a ranking.
- FITC and DTC objectives can *exceed* the exact lml of the same data at some parameters, whereas the VFE bound never does (tested), which is why VFE can be used for model comparison and the others cannot.

Inducing placement at M=12, hypers fixed at the exact fit, 5 starts:

| placement | mean VFE bound | mean tr(Kff - Qff) |
|---|---|---|
| greedy (pivoted Cholesky) | -1325 | 78 |
| random data points | -2328 | 219 |
| k-means centres | -1265 | 58 |

Greedy is far better than random, but k-means centres, which need not be data points, beat it here: on a dense 1-D design the best inducing inputs sit between observations.

### 3. Random features versus Nystrom at equal feature count (`features`)

n=400 points in 2-D from an RBF GP, hypers fixed at the truth so only the approximation differs; 5 seeds. "Err" is the relative Frobenius error of `Phi Phi^T`; "gap" the relative L2 distance of the predictive mean from the exact GP's.

| D | RFF err | ORF err | Nystrom (random) err | Nystrom (greedy) err | RFF pred gap | Nystrom greedy gap | RFF var ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.783 | 0.722 | 0.255 | 0.220 | 0.437 | 0.427 | 0.31 |
| 25 | 0.467 | 0.490 | 0.044 | 0.010 | 0.239 | 0.049 | 0.51 |
| 50 | 0.320 | 0.318 | 0.003 | 0.000 | 0.129 | 0.001 | 0.64 |
| 100 | 0.229 | 0.229 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.083 | 0.000 | 0.73 |
| 200 | 0.184 | 0.131 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.063 | 0.000 | 0.81 |
| 500 | 0.101 | 0.097 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.040 | 0.000 | 0.91 |

- RFF error falls like `1/sqrt(D)` (0.78 to 0.10 over 50x the features), while **data-dependent Nystrom is exact to three decimals by D=50** on a smooth 2-D kernel. Random features are data-independent, which is the price; their appeal is that they do not need the training set to be built and give a fixed-size map for streaming or distributed fitting.
- **RFF predictive variance is too small** (ratio 0.31 at D=10 to 0.91 at D=500): with few features the posterior cannot be uncertain in directions it cannot represent ("variance starvation"). A GP built on too few random features is *overconfident* away from data even when its mean is decent.
- Orthogonal features helped at D=200 (0.131 vs 0.184) and were within noise elsewhere in 2-D; the unit test where they win clearly uses 4-D inputs and 8 features.

### 4. Calibration and model selection (`calibration`)

Train n=80, test 400, 20 seeds, noise variance 0.05. Each cell fits RBF, Matern 3/2 and Matern 5/2 (3 starts) to data from one true family. Nominal 95% intervals (noise included):

| truth | model | 95% coverage | NLPD | mean lml | mean LOO log-pred |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| rbf | rbf | 0.936 | 0.005 | -12.5 | 3.8 |
| rbf | matern32 | 0.937 | 0.021 | -13.8 | 2.8 |
| rbf | matern52 | 0.937 | 0.011 | -13.0 | 3.3 |
| matern32 | rbf | 0.935 | 0.122 | -26.1 | -5.9 |
| matern32 | matern32 | 0.936 | 0.103 | -25.0 | -4.5 |
| matern32 | matern52 | 0.936 | 0.106 | -25.2 | -4.8 |
| matern52 | rbf | 0.937 | 0.059 | -20.0 | -0.9 |
| matern52 | matern32 | 0.936 | 0.057 | -19.7 | -0.8 |
| matern52 | matern52 | 0.936 | 0.053 | -19.5 | -0.6 |

Fraction of seeds in which the marginal likelihood (LOO) selects the true family, chance 0.33: RBF 0.75 (0.85), Matern 3/2 0.55 (0.55), **Matern 5/2 0.15 (0.20)**.

- Coverage is 0.935-0.937 against a nominal 0.95 for every model: slightly over-confident because the hyper-parameters are estimated from 80 points, and **not** sensitive to getting the kernel family wrong.
- Likelihood and LOO point the right way on average (the true family has the best mean lml in all three rows) but a *single* data set of 80 points identifies Matern 5/2 worse than chance, most likely because RBF and Matern 5/2 are close (the 0.15 is measured, the reason is not tested). Closed-form LOO costs nothing extra and picks the truth slightly more often for RBF (0.85 vs 0.75) and equally often otherwise, so it is a cheap second opinion rather than a stronger detector.

### 5. Structure beats flexibility when extrapolating (`extrapolation`)

n=160 noisy points of `0.35 t + (1 + 0.3 sin(2 pi t / 30)) sin(2 pi t)` on `t in [0, 12]`; scored on the noise-free truth over `[0, 12)` and `[12, 16]`. Periodic kernels are fit from six initial periods (0.4 to 4) and keep the best marginal likelihood.

| kernel | lml | params | RMSE interp | RMSE extrap | f coverage extrap | period |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| rbf | -225.9 | 3 | 0.869 | 1.499 | 0.58 | - |
| matern32 + linear | -145.3 | 4 | 0.191 | 2.174 | 0.89 | - |
| periodic + linear | -79.3 | 5 | 0.078 | 0.143 | 0.74 | 1.000 |
| periodic * rbf + linear | -78.3 | 7 | 0.057 | 0.171 | 1.00 | 1.000 |

Marginal likelihood ranks both periodic kernels far above the other two (about 65 nats), and the structured kernels find the true period (1.000) from the likelihood alone; between the two periodic models the likelihood prefers the locally periodic one by 1 nat while the simpler one extrapolates slightly better. The flexible kernels fail outside the data: a Matern 3/2 + linear fit interpolates (0.19) but extrapolates worse than the plain RBF because it cannot continue the oscillation. The two periodic models also differ in calibration: the simpler one is overconfident on the extrapolation (74% of the truth inside its 95% band), the locally periodic one is conservative (100%) and slightly worse in RMSE (0.171 vs 0.143).

### 6. Cost (`scaling`)

One objective evaluation (exact: lml + gradient) plus prediction at 500 points, 2-D inputs, median of 3 on one core; VFE with M=50 (inducing selection excluded), RFF with D=200:

| n | exact | VFE M=50 | RFF D=200 | exact memory | VFE memory |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 250 | 7.5 ms | 1.1 ms | 6.0 ms | 0.5 MB | 0.1 MB |
| 500 | 34.5 ms | 1.3 ms | 7.1 ms | 2.0 MB | 0.2 MB |
| 1000 | 200.6 ms | 1.9 ms | 9.7 ms | 8.0 MB | 0.4 MB |
| 2000 | 1306.9 ms | 3.0 ms | 14.8 ms | 32.0 MB | 0.8 MB |

Exact cost grows by 4.6x to 6.5x per doubling and is still approaching the cubic 8x; VFE and RFF grow roughly linearly in n. At n=2000 VFE is about 440x faster.

## Limitations

- Regression with a Gaussian likelihood only (no classification, no Student-t noise); constant mean.
- Sparse hyper-parameters use finite-difference gradients with `Z` fixed (not learned inducing locations); the sparse optimiser is started from one point, so a local optimum is possible and one study cell is one run.
- `Periodic` is a product of 1-D periodic kernels with a shared period (the Euclidean-distance form is not PSD beyond 1-D).
- Exact GP uses a dense inverse of the Cholesky factor for clarity, so it is for n of a few thousand at most.
- Synthetic data only; the studies show behaviours of the methods, not benchmark rankings.

## v0.2: GP classification (Laplace)

`gplab.classify.LaplaceGPC` implements binary GP classification with a logistic likelihood
(Rasmussen & Williams Algs. 3.1-3.2): damped Newton mode finding in the stable
`B = I + W^1/2 K W^1/2` form, the Laplace approximate log marginal likelihood, and predictive
probabilities via the probit approximation (checked against Gauss-Hermite quadrature).
Hyper-parameters are fit with L-BFGS on central finite-difference gradients of the approximate
marginal likelihood. `log_loss` and `expected_calibration_error` are included.

```python
from gplab import RBF, LaplaceGPC
clf = LaplaceGPC(RBF(0.5, 2.0)).fit(X, y01)
p = clf.predict_proba(Xs)
```
Tests (6 new, 68 total) verify the mode is a stationary point of the penalised likelihood, beats
perturbations, the LML matches exact 1-D integration in the Gaussian limit, and that fitting improves LML on a synthetic two-moons set.

- `SpectralMixture` (v0.3): Gaussian-mixture spectral density kernel with analytic gradients (per-dimension product). Tests: finite-difference gradients, PSD, reduces to RBF at zero mean, and on a two-frequency signal (0.5 and 1.3 Hz, synthetic) extrapolates 8 to 10 with RMSE < 0.15 and under half of an RBF GP's; L-BFGS from a rough init recovers both frequencies within 0.03. The likelihood is multimodal, so initialise means from a periodogram or use restarts.

## ARD feature relevance (v0.3.1)

`gplab.ard` fits an ARD-RBF GP by marginal likelihood and ranks inputs by inverse lengthscale
(`fit_ard`, `relevance`, `rank_features`). Tests check that on a 5-input synthetic function
with 2 informative inputs, those two are ranked first and ARD beats an isotropic kernel on held-out MSE.

## SVGP (v0.4)

`SVGP` trains a sparse variational GP with natural-gradient minibatch steps (Hensman et al. 2013) in the whitened parametrisation. For Gaussian likelihoods each step is a convex blend of the current natural parameters with the minibatch estimate of the optimum; at rho=1 on the full batch it reproduces the collapsed VFE predictor and bound exactly (tested to 1e-6). Hyperparameters and Z are fixed. Cost per step is O(b m^2 + m^3).

```python
from gplab import RBF, SVGP, kmeans_inducing
gp = SVGP(RBF(0.9, 1.2), kmeans_inducing(X, 12), noise=0.1).fit(X, y, epochs=60, batch=40)
mean, var = gp.predict(Xs)
```

## v0.5: robust (Student-t) regression

`RobustGP(kernel, noise, nu)` fits a Student-t likelihood by EM on its Gaussian scale-mixture form
(per-point noise `noise / w_i`, closed-form weight updates, kernel hyper-parameters held fixed).
`weights_` / `outliers()` expose which points were down-weighted. With 6 of 60 points shifted by 2-4
units, the test suite requires the robust fit to have under 0.6x the function RMSE of a Gaussian GP
at the same hyper-parameters, and the corrupted points' weights to sit far below the clean median
(`tests/test_robust.py`). Hyper-parameters are not learned; the EM objective can have poor local modes
if `noise` is set far too small.

## v0.6: heteroscedastic regression

`HeteroGP(kernel_f, kernel_g)` is the most-likely heteroscedastic GP (Kersting et al. 2007) with fixed
kernels: it alternates a GP on the mean with per-point noise `exp(g)` and a GP on the log of squared
leave-one-out residuals (closed-form LOO, bias-corrected by E[log chi2_1]) to estimate `g`. Tests on
a sine with noise sd rising from 0.05 to 0.45 require log-noise correlation > 0.8 with the truth, lower
held-out NLPD than a homoscedastic GP, and 95% coverage between 0.88 and 0.99. With constant noise the
estimated noise stays roughly flat. Hyper-parameters are not learned; this is a point estimate of g, not a full posterior.
